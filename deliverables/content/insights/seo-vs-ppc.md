# SEO vs. PPC: How to Actually Decide

"Should we do SEO or PPC?" is usually the wrong question. They solve different problems, on different timescales, with different cost structures. The useful question is which one your situation calls for first — and that answer is determined by your margin, your sales cycle, and whether anyone is searching for what you sell yet.

### Key takeaways

- PPC buys visibility immediately and stops the moment you stop paying. SEO compounds slowly and persists.
- The choice is driven by unit economics and time horizon, not by preference.
- If nobody is searching for your category yet, neither channel will work — you have a demand problem, not a channel problem.
- Run together, each makes the other cheaper and more informative.

## What each one actually is

**SEO** improves your position in unpaid search results through content, technical health and earned links. Traffic is free at the margin but the work is not, and results arrive over months.

**PPC** places paid ads on results pages. You pay per click, appear immediately, and stop appearing the moment the budget stops.

Both target search intent. The difference is how you pay for it — capital expenditure that accumulates versus operating expenditure that recurs.

## The honest comparison

| | SEO | PPC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time to first result | Months | Hours |
| Cost structure | Front-loaded, then near-zero per click | Per click, forever |
| Persistence | Traffic continues when spend pauses | Traffic stops when spend stops |
| Targeting precision | Query-level only | Query, geography, device, audience, time |
| Testing speed | Slow — weeks per iteration | Fast — days |
| Competitive vulnerability | Algorithm updates | Bid inflation |

Two entries deserve more than a table cell.

**Persistence** is SEO's real argument. A page that ranks keeps delivering after the work stops. Paid traffic is rented; the moment the card declines, it is gone. Businesses that have run paid-only for years often discover they have built no asset at all.

**Testing speed** is PPC's underrated argument, and it is worth more than most people assign to it. A PPC campaign tells you in a fortnight which messages convert, which segments buy, and what language people use — intelligence that would take six months to extract from organic. Running paid first to learn, then building content around what proved to work, is usually a better sequence than the reverse.

## What should actually drive the decision

### Your margin per customer

PPC only works if you can afford the click. In competitive categories a single click can cost more than a low-margin product earns. If your contribution margin cannot absorb the cost per acquisition at realistic conversion rates, paid search is not viable at any level of optimisation — and no amount of account management fixes it.

Do this arithmetic before launching, not after.

### Your time horizon

Need revenue this quarter? PPC. Building a durable position over two years? SEO. Both, if you have the budget — paid for now, organic for later.

A business with runway measured in months should not be investing primarily in a channel that produces results in a year.

### Whether demand exists

Both channels capture existing search demand. Neither creates it.

If you have launched something genuinely new, or a project nobody has heard of, search volume for it is zero. Bidding on it reaches nobody; ranking for it ranks for nothing. That is a demand generation problem, solved with social, video, display or PR — and only once that has run does search have anything to capture.

Accounts get blamed for this constantly. The campaign is not broken; the demand does not exist yet.

### Your competitive position

If established competitors dominate organic results in your category, ranking will take a long time and may not be achievable at your resourcing. PPC buys you into the conversation immediately while organic builds — or, sometimes, instead of organic building.

## Where PPC is clearly right

- You need results now.
- You are testing a new offer, market or message.
- Demand is seasonal or event-driven and you need to be present in a specific window.
- Margins comfortably absorb the click cost.
- The keywords you need are ones you will not realistically rank for.

## Where SEO is clearly right

- You are building for the long term and can wait.
- Your category has real informational search volume you can serve.
- Click costs in your category are prohibitive.
- You want an asset that persists rather than a cost that recurs.

## Why running both is usually better

The two channels are not independent, and the interactions are worth more than most teams realise.

**Paid search data improves organic strategy.** The search term report shows you exactly which queries convert. That is the best possible input to a content plan — real conversion data rather than keyword tool estimates.

**Organic reduces paid dependence.** As pages rank, you can reduce spend on those terms and redirect it.

**Occupying both positions increases total clicks.** Appearing in the paid slot and the organic results does not simply cannibalise; it typically raises combined click share and reinforces credibility.

**Paid covers organic gaps.** New pages, seasonal pushes and terms you cannot rank for get covered by paid until — or unless — organic catches up.

## A practical sequence

For most businesses starting from nothing:

1. **Fix the fundamentals first.** Site speed, mobile experience, conversion tracking, and a landing page that works. Both channels fail without these, and both will be blamed for it.
2. **Run paid to learn.** A modest budget on tightly targeted terms tells you within weeks what converts and at what cost.
3. **Build content around what proved to work.** You now have evidence rather than assumptions.
4. **Reduce paid where organic takes hold** — and reinvest the saving in terms organic will not reach.

## Conclusion

The decision is not ideological. Work out what a customer is worth to you, how long you can wait, and whether anyone is searching yet. Those three answers determine the split.

If you can only fund one and need revenue soon, run paid — and use it to learn, so the organic work you do later is aimed at something you have already proved converts.

## FAQ

### Which is cheaper?

SEO has lower marginal cost per visit but higher upfront investment and a long delay. PPC costs more per click but produces results immediately. Over several years SEO is usually cheaper per visit; in the first quarter it delivers nothing.

### Can I do just SEO?

Yes, if you have the time horizon and your category has meaningful organic search volume. You will be slower to learn what converts.

### Can I do just PPC?

Yes, if your margins support it — but understand you are renting traffic permanently and building no asset.

### Does running PPC help my organic rankings?

Not directly; paid spend is not a ranking factor. It helps indirectly by revealing which queries convert, which is the best input to a content strategy.

### How much should I budget for PPC?

Enough that campaigns gather meaningful conversion data. A budget spread too thin across too many keywords produces no learning and no results. Start narrow and well-funded rather than broad and starved.

### Why is neither channel working for my new product?

Possibly because nobody is searching for it. Search captures demand; it does not create it. If your category or product has no search volume, you need demand generation first.
