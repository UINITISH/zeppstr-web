# If your agency's case study claims more revenue than the project was worth, stop reading it

Here is a test you can run on any agency deck in under a minute.

Find the claimed revenue. Find the total value of the thing being sold. Divide.

We recently reviewed a real-estate marketing deck that claimed ₹4,555 crore of attributed revenue on a project with a stated total value of ₹450 crore. Another case study in the same document claimed ₹2,051 crore on a ₹280 crore project. Across five clients, the deck totalled ₹12,674 crore of attributed revenue and an average ROI of 1,897%.

You cannot sell more real estate than the project contains. The numbers were not aggressive. They were impossible.

## Why this happens, and it usually isn't fraud

Almost nobody sets out to fabricate. The mechanism is more mundane.

Someone needs a case study. The campaign data exists — impressions, leads, cost per lead, all real. But leads are not a boardroom number, and somebody senior wants a revenue figure. So a chain of estimates gets built: leads × assumed close rate × average ticket value = revenue. Each link sounds defensible. Nobody sanity-checks the product against the size of the asset.

Then the number gets repeated. It goes in a deck, the deck becomes a PDF, the PDF becomes the website. By the time anyone questions it, it has been said out loud enough times to feel true.

## What it costs you

Not a lawsuit, usually. Something slower.

The buyer for a high-value marketing engagement is frequently a CFO or a founder who thinks in unit economics. They do the division automatically — it is a reflex, the way a copywriter notices a typo. And the moment the arithmetic fails, they are no longer evaluating your marketing capability. They are evaluating your relationship with facts.

You do not get told this. You get "we've decided to go in a different direction."

The cruellest part is that the underlying work is usually genuinely good. In that same deck, the operational metrics were strong and entirely credible: lead volume up meaningfully, cost per lead down 34–42%, ROAS improving from roughly 2× to 3.5–4×. Any competent CMO would be impressed. Those numbers got buried under a revenue claim that torched the credibility of everything around them.

## What to claim instead

**Claim what you control.** You control cost per lead, lead volume, funnel conversion, ROAS. You do not control the sales team, the inventory, the pricing committee or the market. Claiming revenue means claiming credit for their work too.

**State your assumptions in the open.** "₹34 crore of pipeline, modelled at a 1% lead-to-booking close rate — the conservative floor for premium real estate" is a defensible sentence. "₹34 crore in revenue generated" is not the same claim, and everyone reading knows it.

**Name the ceiling.** If the project is worth ₹450 crore, your attributable pipeline cannot exceed it. Write that constraint down before you write the number.

**Refuse the number you can't source.** Declining to claim attributed revenue reads as confidence, not modesty — precisely because so few agencies do it.

## The one-minute audit

Open your own case studies. For each revenue or ROI claim, ask three questions:

1. What is the total addressable value of what the client was selling?
2. Does my claim exceed it, or approach it implausibly?
3. If a CFO asked me to show the working, could I — from platform data, not from a chain of assumptions?

If any answer is uncomfortable, that case study is costing you deals right now. You just cannot see which ones.
