# Eighty-six percent of your leads come from five ads. The skill is deleting the rest.

Most paid social accounts do not have a creative problem. They have a retirement problem.

In a three-month period on a luxury real estate account we ran this year, thirty-three Meta creatives were tested. Two survived to carry the account. By the final month, the top five creatives produced 86% of every lead the platform generated — and the single best performer, a static floor-plan image, delivered 440 leads at ₹1,378 per enquiry. That was 37% below the account's three-month blended average.

A static image. Not a reel, not a carousel, not a production. One picture of a floor plan outdelivered thirty-two other ads.

## The uncomfortable part

The team did not make that image better than the others. They could not have predicted it. Anyone who tells you they can reliably pick the winner in advance is selling something.

What the team did was retire the other thirty-one without ceremony.

That is the actual discipline, and it is much harder than it sounds — because every retired creative represents someone's work. Somebody wrote it, somebody designed it, somebody defended it in a review. Killing it feels like waste. Keeping it feels like thrift.

It is the reverse. Every underperforming creative left running is a live tax on the account. It absorbs budget the algorithm would otherwise route to the winner, it dilutes the learning signal, and it flattens your results toward the mean at precisely the moment you want them concentrated.

## Why agencies rarely do this

Because retainer economics quietly punish it.

If you are paid to produce twelve creatives a month, recommending that nine be switched off after five days is an argument against your own invoice. The incentive is to keep everything running, report on "volume of assets delivered," and describe the blended average as the result.

We paused a Demand Gen campaign on that same account inside twenty-one days. It had produced four leads at ₹8,756 each against a blended ₹2,810. There was no ambiguity in the data — only an awkward conversation about work already paid for.

## What to actually do

**Set the kill date before you launch.** A creative that has not found signal by Day 5 will not find it on Day 12. Decide that in advance, when nobody is emotionally invested, and write it down.

**Concentrate deliberately, not accidentally.** On the account above, by the final month the top four creatives were absorbing 92% of spend. That was a decision, not a drift.

**Judge the portfolio, not the asset.** The question is never "is this ad good?" It is "does this ad earn its slot against the current champion?" Those produce different answers, and only the second one compounds.

**Count what you retired.** If your account has thirty active creatives and no retirement log, you do not have a testing programme. You have an archive.

## The thing nobody says out loud

Creative concentration feels like risk. Putting 92% of spend behind four assets sounds fragile compared to spreading it across thirty.

It is the opposite. Thirty mediocre creatives is thirty ways to pay above your best available cost per lead. Four proven ones is a known unit economic you can scale against.

The account's best month was its most concentrated month. That is not a coincidence, and it is not unique to real estate.
