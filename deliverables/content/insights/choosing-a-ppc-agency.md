# How to Choose a PPC Agency

We are an agency. Read this with that in mind — and then use it on us.

Most published guidance on choosing a PPC agency is written by agencies, ranks agencies who paid to be ranked, and lists criteria vague enough that everyone passes. This is the version we would want a client to apply, because the questions that actually separate competent shops from expensive ones are uncomfortable to answer.

### Key takeaways

- Own your ad account. An agency that runs your campaigns inside its own account holds your data hostage.
- Percentage-of-spend fees pay the agency to spend more, not to spend well.
- Ask what they would *stop* doing. Anyone who only wants to add is selling, not diagnosing.
- If reporting stops at clicks and impressions, they are not managing to business outcomes.

## The four questions that matter most

### 1. "Whose Google Ads account will this live in?"

**The right answer:** yours. You own the account; they get access as a user.

**Why it matters:** if campaigns run inside the agency's account, then when you leave, you leave without your conversion history, your negative keyword lists, your audience segments and your Quality Score history. That history is worth real money — a new account starts learning from zero, which costs weeks and budget to rebuild.

Some agencies structure it this way for genuine operational convenience. It still means the switching cost is yours, not theirs. Get account ownership in writing before signing.

### 2. "How is your fee calculated?"

**Percentage of ad spend** is the most common model and the worst aligned. It pays the agency more when you spend more, regardless of what the spend produces. The agency that tells you to cut budget on a failing campaign is arguing itself out of revenue.

**Flat retainer** is neutral. The agency earns the same whether spend goes up or down, which at least removes the perverse incentive.

**Performance-linked** aligns best but needs care — tie it to qualified leads or revenue, never to clicks, conversions-as-counted-by-the-platform, or any metric the agency controls the definition of.

Ask directly what happens to their fee if they recommend halving your budget. The answer tells you a lot.

### 3. "What would you stop doing in our current account?"

An agency that has genuinely audited your account will have found waste — irrelevant search terms, campaigns that have never converted, budget going to branded terms you would have won anyway.

An agency that only proposes additions has not looked, or has looked and would rather sell you more.

This question also exposes whether they understand that most PPC improvement comes from removal rather than expansion.

### 4. "How will you know if this is working, and when will you tell me it isn't?"

You are looking for cost per *qualified* lead and revenue — not impressions, click-through rate, or conversions the platform reports without CRM verification.

More importantly, you want a stated point at which they will tell you it is not working. An agency with no failure condition will report success indefinitely.

## What to check before the first call

**Their own advertising.** Are they bidding on their own category terms? Does their landing page do the things they will tell you to do?

**Their case studies.** Divide claimed revenue by the plausible total value of what was being sold. If the arithmetic does not survive that, neither should the claim. We have written about [why this test matters](/insights) — impossible ROI figures are surprisingly common in agency decks.

**Named clients.** Vague references to "a leading e-commerce brand" usually mean they cannot name anyone. Ask who, and ask to speak to one.

**Who actually does the work.** The person in the pitch is often not the person managing the account. Ask who your day-to-day contact will be and what else they are running.

## What to check on a reference call

Speak to a current client, and one who has left if you can get the name.

- Did results match what was promised at the pitch?
- How quickly do they respond when something breaks?
- Have they ever told you to spend less?
- Did they explain a failure honestly, or was it always the market?
- Would you sign again?

The "have they ever told you to spend less" question is the most revealing one on the list.

## Category experience: how much it matters

Less than agencies claim, more than nothing.

What genuinely transfers between categories: account structure, negative keyword discipline, conversion tracking, bidding strategy, landing page principles.

What does not: knowing that property enquiries decay within hours, that education has admissions cycles, that healthcare advertising carries regulatory constraints, that B2B leads need CRM-stage feedback to be optimised against at all.

Ask what is different about running paid media in your category. A generic answer means they will learn it on your budget.

## Warning signs

**Guaranteed results.** Nobody controls the auction, your competitors, or your conversion rate. Guarantees are either meaningless or dishonest.

**No access to the account.** You should be able to log in and see everything, whenever you want.

**Reporting that only goes up.** Real accounts have bad months. A report that never contains bad news is not a report.

**Long lock-ins with no performance clause.** Twelve months with no exit is the agency protecting itself from its own performance.

**Setup fees with nothing to show for them.** Ask what specifically the setup fee buys and what you keep if you leave.

**Cheapest quote by a wide margin.** PPC management is labour. A rate that undercuts everyone usually means an offshore team running templated campaigns across dozens of accounts, or a junior with no supervision.

## What a fair engagement looks like

- You own the ad account and all associated data.
- Fees are flat or performance-linked, not a percentage of spend.
- Reporting covers cost per qualified lead and revenue, monthly, in a call rather than only a PDF.
- Conversion tracking is verified against your CRM in the first month, before optimisation begins.
- There is a defined review point — usually 90 days — with a stated definition of success.
- Notice period is reasonable, and you keep everything.

## Doing it in-house instead

Worth considering honestly. Agencies are worth their fee when the account is complex, the spend is large enough that expertise pays for itself, or you have nobody who can own it.

For a small, simple account with modest spend, the fee can exceed the improvement an agency would produce. In that case, learning it yourself and buying occasional audit help is often better economics.

An agency worth hiring will tell you this when it is true.

## Conclusion

Own your account. Avoid percentage-of-spend fees. Ask what they would stop doing, and what would make them tell you it is not working. Check their arithmetic on their own case studies.

If an agency is uncomfortable with those questions, that discomfort is the answer.

## FAQ

### Should the agency or I own the Google Ads account?

You. Your conversion history, negative lists and audience data are the asset. Get ownership in writing before signing.

### What fee structure is fairest?

Flat retainer or performance-linked against qualified leads or revenue. Percentage of ad spend pays the agency to increase your budget.

### How much should PPC management cost?

It varies with account complexity and spend. Be more suspicious of the cheapest quote than the most expensive one — this is labour, and unusually cheap usually means templated work.

### Does the agency need experience in my industry?

The mechanics transfer between categories; the specifics do not. Ask what is different about your category. A generic answer means they will learn on your budget.

### What are the biggest warning signs?

Guaranteed results, no account access, reporting that never contains bad news, and long lock-ins with no performance clause.

### Should I hire an agency at all?

If your account is simple and your spend is small, the fee may exceed the gain. A good agency will say so.
