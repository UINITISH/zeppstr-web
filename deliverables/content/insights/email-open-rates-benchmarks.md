# Email Open Rates by Industry: Why the Benchmarks Stopped Working

Search for email open rate benchmarks and you will find dozens of tables, all confident, none agreeing. One says retail averages 24%. Another says 18%. A third says 21%. Same metric, same year, same industry.

They disagree because the number they are measuring stopped meaning what it used to mean, and most published benchmarks have not caught up.

### Key takeaways

- Apple's Mail Privacy Protection inflates open rates by pre-loading images whether or not anyone read the email.
- Open rates are no longer comparable between senders, between periods, or against published benchmarks.
- Compare against your own historical baseline, not against an industry table.
- Move your decision-making to clicks, conversions and revenue per recipient.

## What broke

Open rate was never measured directly. It was inferred from a one-pixel tracking image: if the image loaded, the email was assumed to have been opened.

In 2021 Apple introduced Mail Privacy Protection, which pre-loads that image for Apple Mail users regardless of whether the message was ever read. Users are prompted to enable it during device setup, and most do.

The result is that a substantial and *unknown* share of your recorded opens never happened. Not a fixed share — it depends on how much of your list uses Apple Mail on Apple devices, which varies enormously by audience, market and device mix.

That variability is what makes benchmarks useless. If two brands report a 25% open rate but one has an audience that is 60% Apple Mail and the other 15%, the numbers describe entirely different realities.

## Why published benchmarks conflict

Beyond the privacy issue, benchmark tables differ because of choices nobody discloses:

- **Different denominators.** Delivered messages, or messages sent? Bounces materially change the figure.
- **Different customer bases.** A platform's benchmark reflects its own customers, who are not representative of the industry.
- **Different definitions of "industry".** Retail includes both a corner shop's newsletter and a large e-commerce operation.
- **Different periods.** Pre- and post-2021 figures are not comparable, and many tables silently blend them.
- **Survivorship.** Senders who stopped sending because it was not working are absent from the data.

A published benchmark is a description of one platform's customers over some period, presented as an industry standard. Treat it accordingly.

## What to do instead

### Benchmark against yourself

Your own trend line is the only comparison that controls for audience, list quality, sending practice and device mix.

Establish a baseline over several months. Then look at movement — is this campaign above or below your own normal? That question is answerable and actionable. "Are we above the industry average?" is neither.

### Move your decisions to clicks and conversions

Clicks are a real user action. They can be inflated by security scanners, but far less than opens are inflated by pre-loading, and the distortion is more consistent.

The metrics worth managing to:

- **Click-through rate** — genuine engagement.
- **Click-to-open rate** — useful with the same caveat as opens; treat directionally.
- **Conversion rate** — did the email do its job?
- **Revenue per recipient** — the honest measure for commercial email, and the one that makes a small engaged list visibly better than a large inert one.
- **Unsubscribe and complaint rate** — early warning on relevance and cadence.

Revenue per recipient deserves particular attention. It is unaffected by the tracking problem entirely, and it aligns your reporting with what the business cares about.

### Keep open rate for one purpose

Open rate is still useful for **relative comparison within a single send** — an A/B test where both variants go to comparable audiences at the same time, so the inflation applies equally to both.

Even then, prefer clicks where the volume allows. But if you are testing subject lines on a small list, opens are the only signal you have, and equal inflation across both arms makes the comparison usable.

## What still influences whether people open

The underlying behaviour has not changed, even though our ability to measure it has.

**Sender name.** Often more decisive than the subject line. People decide who is worth reading before they read what it says. Use a consistent, recognisable sender.

**Subject line and preview text.** They work together. Preview text is the most under-used field in email — many senders leave it defaulting to "View in browser".

**Relevance.** A well-segmented email to a small group beats a clever subject line sent to everyone.

**Timing and cadence.** Both matter, but less than most people assume, and both are testable against your own audience rather than against published "best times".

**Sender reputation.** An email in the spam folder cannot be opened. Deliverability precedes every other consideration and is invisible in open rate data.

## The trends that actually matter

**Personalisation** works when it changes what is sent, not just the greeting. Behavioural triggers outperform merge tags substantially.

**Mobile-first design** is non-negotiable; most opens are on a phone.

**Automation and lifecycle sequences** consistently outperform broadcast campaigns, because they arrive when the recipient is doing something relevant.

**AI-assisted subject line generation** is useful for producing variants to test. It does not tell you which will work for your audience — only your own testing does that.

## How to report honestly

When reporting to a client or a board:

1. **Lead with revenue and conversions.** Unaffected by the tracking problem.
2. **Report clicks as your engagement measure.**
3. **Include open rate with a caveat**, or exclude it. If it is included, state that it is inflated by privacy protection and not comparable to external figures.
4. **Compare to your own prior periods**, never to a published industry table.
5. **Show list health** — bounce rate, unsubscribe rate, complaint rate — because those determine whether any of the rest continues to work.

A report that omits open rate and explains why is more credible than one that quotes a benchmark it cannot substantiate.

## Conclusion

Open rate is no longer a reliable measure and no longer comparable between senders. Published industry benchmarks conflict because they are describing different populations with different device mixes using different definitions.

Compare against your own history. Manage to clicks, conversions and revenue per recipient. Use open rate only for within-send comparisons where the distortion applies equally to both sides.

## FAQ

### What is a good email open rate?

There is no longer a meaningful universal answer. Compare against your own historical baseline rather than a published figure.

### Why did my open rate jump in 2021?

Almost certainly Apple's Mail Privacy Protection, which pre-loads tracking images and registers opens that did not happen.

### Are industry benchmarks useless?

For decision-making, largely yes. They can be a rough sanity check that you are in a plausible range, but they cannot tell you whether your campaign worked.

### What should I measure instead?

Click-through rate, conversion rate, revenue per recipient, and list health metrics. Revenue per recipient is the most honest single number for commercial email.

### Can I still A/B test subject lines?

Yes. Within one send to comparable audiences the inflation applies equally to both arms, so the comparison holds. Prefer clicks as the metric where volume allows.

### How do I improve opens genuinely?

Consistent recognisable sender name, better segmentation, useful preview text, and — before any of that — deliverability, since an email in the spam folder cannot be opened.
