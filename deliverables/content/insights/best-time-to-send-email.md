# The Best Time to Send Marketing Emails

Every published answer to this question contradicts every other one. Tuesday at 10am. Thursday afternoon. Evenings. Weekends, because inboxes are quieter.

They cannot all be right, and the reason they disagree is instructive: send time matters less than the industry pretends, and what little it does matter is specific to your audience in a way no published table can capture.

### Key takeaways

- Send time is a real but small effect. Relevance and segmentation move results far more.
- Published "best time" studies contradict each other because they describe different audiences and rely on a broken metric.
- Test against your own list, using clicks rather than opens.
- Per-subscriber send-time optimisation, where your platform offers it, beats any universal rule.

## Why the published answers conflict

**They measure open rate.** Since Apple's Mail Privacy Protection began pre-loading tracking images, recorded open time often reflects when a device fetched the image, not when a person read the message. Timing studies built on open data are measuring device behaviour.

**They describe different audiences.** A study of e-commerce consumers and a study of B2B software buyers will produce different answers, and both will be correct for their own population.

**They rarely control for send volume.** If most senders send Tuesday morning, Tuesday morning is both the most competitive slot and the one with the most data.

**Time zones are frequently ignored.** "10am" means nothing for a list spanning several zones unless the platform is sending locally.

## What actually determines whether someone engages

In descending order of impact:

1. **Whether the message is relevant to them.** A well-segmented email at a mediocre time beats a generic one at the perfect time, and it is not close.
2. **Whether it reached the inbox at all.** Deliverability precedes timing entirely.
3. **Whether they recognise and trust the sender.**
4. **Whether the subject line gives a reason to open.**
5. **When it arrived.**

Send time is on the list. It is last for a reason. Teams that spend weeks optimising send time while sending the same message to their whole list have the priority inverted.

## Where timing genuinely matters

Two cases where it matters a great deal:

**Behavioural triggers.** An abandoned cart email an hour after abandonment performs very differently from one sent the next day. Here timing is not a scheduling question — it is the entire mechanism.

**Time-bound offers.** A sale ending at midnight, an event starting tomorrow. The email has to arrive while acting is still possible.

For routine broadcast sending, the effect is much smaller than the discourse suggests.

## How to find your own answer

### Test properly

- **Split your list randomly**, not by segment. Splitting by signup date or alphabetically introduces a variable you did not intend to test.
- **Send the same content**, varying only time.
- **Measure clicks, not opens.** Opens are unreliable for exactly the reason above.
- **Repeat.** One test tells you about one week. Run it three or four times before concluding anything.
- **Test meaningfully different times.** 10am versus 11am is unlikely to resolve. Morning versus evening might.

### Look at your own existing data first

Before running tests, examine what you already have. Across your last six months of sends, is there a pattern in click rate by day and hour?

Be careful: if you always send Tuesday morning, you have no data about Thursday evening, and what looks like a pattern may just be your own habit reflected back.

### Use per-subscriber optimisation

Most established platforms now offer send-time optimisation that learns when each individual subscriber engages and delivers to each of them at their own best moment.

This is straightforwardly better than any universal rule, because it stops looking for a single answer to a question that has a different answer for every recipient. If your platform offers it and you have enough history for it to learn from, use it.

### Respect time zones

If your list spans multiple zones, send in each recipient's local time. Most platforms support this. Without it, "9am" is 9am for one group and the middle of the night for another.

## Reasonable starting points

If you have no data at all and need somewhere to begin — and understanding these are starting hypotheses to test, not answers:

- **B2B:** mid-morning on a weekday, avoiding Monday morning and Friday afternoon.
- **B2C:** evenings and weekends often work, because people have time.
- **Transactional and triggered:** immediately. Delay is the only real mistake available.

Then test, and let your own data replace these within a couple of months.

## Frequency matters more than timing

If you are going to spend effort on one scheduling question, make it frequency rather than time of day.

Too frequent produces unsubscribes and spam complaints — and complaint rate is now a threshold metric, with Google and Yahoo expecting bulk senders to stay below 0.3%. Too infrequent means recipients forget who you are, which increases complaints when you do appear.

Consistency also matters in itself. A predictable schedule builds a habit; erratic sending does not, and it damages sender reputation.

Ask subscribers their preferred frequency at signup where you can, and honour it. A preference centre offering "less often" retains subscribers who would otherwise unsubscribe entirely.

## Combining timing with relevance

The genuine gains come from combining decent timing with real segmentation and behavioural triggers:

- Segment so content differs by group rather than only by name.
- Use dynamic content that changes with subscriber behaviour.
- Build triggered sequences — welcome, abandonment, replenishment, re-engagement — that fire on action rather than on a calendar.

A triggered email arriving at the moment of relevance outperforms any broadcast, at any hour, by a wide margin. That is where the effort belongs.

## Conclusion

There is no universal best time. The published answers conflict because they describe different audiences using a metric that no longer measures what it claims to.

Test against your own list using clicks. Use per-subscriber optimisation if you have it. Send in local time zones. Then stop optimising send time and go work on segmentation, which will move your results considerably more.

## FAQ

### What is the best time to send marketing emails?

There is no universal answer. Test against your own audience using clicks as the metric. B2B mid-morning weekdays and B2C evenings are reasonable starting hypotheses, not conclusions.

### Why do published studies disagree?

They measure different audiences, rely on open-rate data distorted by privacy protection, and rarely control for time zones or competitive send volume.

### How much does send time actually matter?

Less than relevance, deliverability or sender recognition. It is a real effect, but a small one for routine sending. For triggered emails it matters enormously.

### Should I use send-time optimisation?

Yes, if your platform offers it and you have enough sending history. Per-subscriber delivery beats any single rule.

### Does day of week matter more than time of day?

Usually yes, and both matter less than whether the content is relevant to the person receiving it.

### How often should I send?

Consistently, at a frequency your list tolerates. Watch unsubscribe and complaint rates — complaint rate above 0.3% will cause deliverability problems with major providers. Offer a frequency preference where you can.
