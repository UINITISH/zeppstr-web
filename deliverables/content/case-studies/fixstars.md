# Fixstars — marketing to engineers who distrust marketing

Client: Fixstars · Tokyo, Japan · High-performance and embedded AI computing.
Public positioning below is taken from the client's own website and is
independently verifiable.

## The situation

Fixstars sells performance engineering — *speed up your embedded AI, push it to
the hardware limit*. The site is bilingual Japanese and English, publishes an
Investor Relations section, and runs live technical webinars on embedded
development.

Three facts about that combination define the marketing problem entirely: the
buyer is an engineer, the company is publicly accountable, and the market is
split across two languages and business cultures.

## The diagnosis

**Engineers do not respond to marketing language.** The audience for embedded AI
optimisation evaluates claims against benchmarks. Adjectives are noise — worse
than noise, they are a signal that the vendor does not understand the problem. The
only persuasive assets in this category are technical: measured results,
reproducible methods, named hardware, real constraints.

Which is exactly why the webinar programme is the right instrument. A live
technical session is a competence demonstration in a way that no landing page can
be. It also self-qualifies: the people who attend a session on embedded
development are engineers with a live problem, not researchers gathering PDFs.

**IR changes the tone of everything.** A listed company cannot market with the
looseness of a startup. Claims flow into an environment where investors read the
same page as customers, and where an unsupportable performance claim is not just a
credibility problem but a disclosure one. Conservatism is a requirement, not a
style choice.

**Two markets, not one.** Japanese and English are not translation variants of the
same funnel. Buying processes, procurement norms, content expectations and the
role of the vendor relationship all differ. Running one strategy through two
languages produces a programme that underperforms in both.

## What this proves

For deep-technical products, the content *is* the proof. A benchmark, a teardown,
a reproducible method — these do the work that a case study does in other
categories, because the audience trusts a number it can verify over a claim it
cannot.

The corollary is uncomfortable for most agencies: you cannot produce credible
marketing in this category without genuine technical input from the client. The
work is not writing about the engineering. It is building the system that gets the
engineering in front of the right engineers, repeatedly.
